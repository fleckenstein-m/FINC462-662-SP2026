Question 1 – Interpreting Yield to Maturity

Which of the following best describes the yield to maturity (YTM) on a coupon bond?




Yield to maturity is defined as the single discount rate that sets the present value of all promised cash flows equal to the bond’s current price; it is an internal rate of return based on holding to maturity.


Question 2 – Zero-Coupon Bonds and YTM

A 20-year zero-coupon Treasury STRIPS has a par value of $100. If its market price falls today while maturity and par value stay the same, what happens to its yield to maturity (YTM), assuming semi-annual compounding?




For a zero-coupon bond, the YTM is determined by the ratio of par value to price; with maturity fixed, a lower price implies a higher implied growth rate and therefore a higher yield to maturity.


Question 3 – Discount Yield vs. Return

Treasury bills are quoted on a discount yield basis. Why is the quoted discount yield on a T-bill not a good measure of the investor’s actual rate of return?




The T-bill discount yield uses the discount divided by par, not by the investor’s actual outlay (the price). This understates the true return on the funds invested and makes it inconsistent with bond yields based on price.


Question 4 – Bond-Equivalent Yield (BEY)

The main purpose of converting a T-bill’s discount yield into a bond-equivalent yield (BEY) is to:




The BEY converts the discount quote into an annualized rate based on the actual price paid, so it can be meaningfully compared with the yield to maturity on coupon-paying Treasury securities of similar maturity.


Question 5 – Premium, Par, and Discount

A 5-year Treasury note with a 4% coupon (paid semi-annually) is currently trading at $104 per $100 of par value. What must be true about the bond’s yield to maturity (YTM)?




A bond trading above par is a premium bond. For a premium bond, the coupon rate exceeds the yield to maturity, so if the coupon is 4%, the YTM must be less than 4%.


Question 6 – Par Yield

The U.S. Treasury wants to issue a new 2-year note at a price of 100 (at par). Given a set of risk‑free discount factors for future cash flows, the coupon rate they choose must equal:




The par yield is defined as the coupon rate that, when applied to a bond of a given maturity and discounted using the observed discount factors, gives a price of exactly 100. New issues priced at par set the coupon equal to this par yield.


Question 7 – Bond Price–Yield Relation

Which statement best describes the price–yield relation for a standard, option‑free fixed‑rate bond?




As yields increase, bond prices fall, and vice versa. The relationship is not linear; the price–yield curve is convex, as shown in the lecture’s price‑yield plot.


Question 8 – Law of One Price

According to the law of one price as applied in fixed‑income markets, if two portfolios of risk‑free bonds have the same cash flows in every period and in every state of the world, then:




The law of one price states that two portfolios with identical payoffs must trade at the same price; otherwise investors can construct an arbitrage strategy by buying the cheaper and short‑selling the more expensive.


Question 9 – Arbitrage with Bond C

In the lecture example, Bond C has the same cash flows as a portfolio consisting of Bond A and Bond B together. Suppose Bond A and Bond B together cost $68, but Bond C costs $71. Ignoring transaction costs, which arbitrage trade is consistent with the law of one price?




Bond C is overpriced relative to the replicating portfolio of A and B (71 > 68). The arbitrage is to short‑sell the expensive asset (Bond C) and buy the cheaper replicating portfolio (Bonds A and B), locking in a risk‑free profit.
